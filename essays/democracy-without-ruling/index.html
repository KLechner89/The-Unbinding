Democracy Without Ruling

Political Agency and Its Displacement at Scale

November 10, 2026

Democracy is usually judged by the opportunities it gives citizens to participate. Elections, public debate, and organized opposition provide indispensable means of holding rulers to account. Their endurance leaves a further question: what authority do citizens exercise over the decisions made in their name? This essay calls the loss of that authority democratic exhaustion. It arises when institutions continue to solicit participation while the power to frame public choices and determine their ends settles elsewhere. Aristotle’s account of citizenship supplies the standard by which this change is assessed: citizens exercise political agency through a shared responsibility for judgment and rule. The accounts of constitutional change and modern government examined here explain how that responsibility is sustained, restricted, and displaced. Democratic exhaustion takes hold where citizens retain political membership and access to procedures while losing an effective part in directing the institutions that govern them.

Aristotle defines citizenship through participation in ruling. In Politics III.1, “The unconditional citizen is defined by nothing else so much as by his participation in judgment and office.”1 This includes people who exercise public judgment without holding a conventional, time-limited magistracy. Jurors and assemblymen count as office-holders because their decisions carry authority over common affairs.2 Citizenship acquires its political substance through the work citizens perform together.

Tocqueville’s later examination of the American jury shows what this responsibility involves in practice. In Democracy in America, the jury is a political institution because it places citizens in a position to judge their fellows. Service temporarily invests each juror with a magistracy and draws him beyond his private affairs. The experience also educates him: judging a case teaches the habits of attention and responsibility on which public judgment depends.3 The judge’s expertise introduces a difficulty within this education. In civil cases that turn on questions of law, his influence extends so far that “the jurors pronounce the ruling that the judge has rendered.”4 The institution places citizens in a position of authority while drawing them into dependence on professional knowledge. Its political value therefore turns on the character of that relationship. Expert instruction develops the citizen’s judgment when it equips him to exercise responsibility; it displaces that judgment when the decision has effectively been made for him.

Aristotle’s criterion helps explain what is at stake in the jury. Citizens share in government by exercising consequential judgment over common affairs. Selecting officeholders is one means of discharging that responsibility, whose significance depends on the authority citizens retain over those they select. Participation loses its governing substance when citizens’ judgments cease to direct the conduct of government.

The capacity to exercise this authority develops through experience. Aristotle describes political rule as “a sort of rule exercised over those who are similar in kind and free,” and holds that “the ruler must learn it by being ruled.”5 His military analogy makes the sequence clear: service under a commander prepares a person to command. Accordingly, “one cannot rule well unless one has been [well] ruled.”6 A political community gives this education a constitutional form. Citizens deliberate, live under decisions made in common, and take responsibility for exercising authority in turn. Through these practices they learn what judgment requires of both ruler and ruled. Institutions that remove citizens from this reciprocal relationship also remove an important part of their political education. The capacity for self-government depends on opportunities to practice it.

The pursuit of advantage puts this reciprocal arrangement under strain. Aristotle observes that “people wish to rule continuously, as if they were sickly and would come to be healthy by always ruling.”7 Office has become something its holders seek to possess. Their continued enjoyment of its benefits takes precedence over the claims of others to rule in turn. This changes the relation between officeholders and the community: those excluded from authority lose opportunities to acquire the judgment its exercise demands. Where offices and assemblies survive under these conditions, their continued existence gives an incomplete picture of political life. The distribution of responsibility has narrowed, and with it the practice through which citizens learn to govern.

Aristotle’s classification of constitutions adds a question of purpose to this account of citizenship. In Politics III.6–7, kingship, aristocracy, and polity govern for the common advantage. Tyranny, oligarchy, and democracy are their corresponding deviations, each serving the advantage of the ruling element.8 Democracy here means rule for the particular advantage of the many, a usage distinct from its modern constitutional meaning. The classification asks both who governs and whose good the government serves. Popular possession of authority must still be judged by the purposes for which that authority is exercised.

His discussion of constitutional identity raises a related difficulty: when a city’s constitution changes, in what sense does the city remain the same?9 For the present inquiry, this question directs attention to changes in governing activity beneath the continuity of political names. A community’s institutions endure through the decisions people make within them. Understanding their transformation therefore requires an account of how the conduct of rulers and citizens changes the distribution and purposes of rule.

Polybius develops such an account in Book VI of the Histories. Kingship deteriorates into tyranny, aristocracy into oligarchy, and democracy into mob rule as the conduct of those holding power changes the character of the constitution.10 His account of Rome’s mixed constitution responds to this vulnerability through the distribution of authority. Consuls, Senate, and people possess powers that require mutual assistance and permit each to obstruct the others.11 These relations give restraint an institutional form. The constitution’s durability depends on its constituent powers continuing to exercise their authority in ways that check one another.

Prosperity places this arrangement under pressure from within. Polybius associates constitutional decline with the extravagance and heightened rivalry for office that follow a long period of success.12 The achievements of a political order alter the conduct of the people who sustain it. Institutions established to secure a common life become objects of ambition, and their practical operation changes with the purposes pursued through them. Polybius makes the internal transformation of a successful constitution a problem in its own right. His account supplies a question that carries into modern government: how do institutions change the effective place of citizens even as they continue to sustain the political order?

Machiavelli approaches the endurance of popular liberty through the pressures within republican life. His Discourses on Livy asks how citizens preserve a political place against those seeking to dominate them. The maintenance of liberty requires institutions that give popular interests an enduring influence over government.

The difficulty begins in the different ends pursued by the great and the people. Machiavelli argues that liberty should be entrusted to those least inclined to usurp it, since “the nobles are very keen to dominate, while the people want only not to be dominated.”13 Their interest in resisting domination makes the people suitable guardians of liberty. Placing this responsibility in their hands gives them a reason to prevent others from seizing the power they have little prospect of securing for themselves. Machiavelli’s criterion here is freedom from domination, which differs from Aristotle’s emphasis on sharing in rule. The two accounts nevertheless meet in their concern with consequential political agency. Popular resistance has governing force when it imposes binding limits on those who hold authority.

Machiavelli locates that force in the conflicts between Rome’s social orders. He describes the clashes between the nobility and the people as “the primary factor making for Rome’s continuing freedom.”14 Their value lay in the laws and offices they produced. As he writes, “all the laws made in order to foster liberty result from the tensions between” the people and the great.15 The tribunes gave popular grievances an institutional consequence by turning opposition into enforceable restraints on elite power. This connection between pressure and law explains why conflict matters to liberty. Opposition that shapes the exercise of office gives citizens leverage over government. Once it is confined to episodic protest or expression, its presence offers much less assurance that those governing will remain subject to popular restraint.

The comparison with Sparta and Venice reveals a cost of suppressing these pressures. Machiavelli describes arrangements that secured internal stability by limiting popular participation. Venice fixed political authority in the hands of those present at its founding and excluded later arrivals from government, closing off a source of conflict between social orders.16 Under such an arrangement, peace reflects the restriction of political access. Those outside the governing body have lost an institutional means of turning their grievances into law. The comparison exposes a tension within the pursuit of stability: measures that reduce internal conflict also weaken the popular leverage through which citizens restrain authority. An orderly republic that secures peace by closing access to government preserves its stability at the expense of popular liberty.

Popular agency depends on maintaining this connection between opposition and binding decisions. Its institutional expression remains vulnerable to changes in the purposes and organization of government. Modern administration adds a further difficulty. Governing large populations requires specialized work whose continuity extends beyond the occasions on which citizens assemble or vote. Weber examines the political consequences of this dependence on officials.

In “Suffrage and Democracy in Germany,” Max Weber argues that “so-called direct democracy is technically possible only in a small state (canton),” and that “in all mass states democracy leads to bureaucratic administration and, without parliamentarization, to pure rule by officials.”17 Mass government relies on officials whose specialized knowledge and continuous work give effect to public decisions. These administrative requirements create a sustained dependence on professional authority. Citizen judgment must work through institutions capable of carrying decisions across extensive populations and over time. The resulting question is who controls the purposes that this organization serves. Weber’s qualification about parliamentarization places that question inside his account of bureaucracy from the outset.

Suffrage gives citizens a means of confronting official power. Under the “inescapable rule of bureaucracy,” Weber writes, the ballot slip becomes “the only instrument of power” capable of granting them a minimal right of co-determination in the affairs of the community.18 His formulation makes voting a necessary source of leverage in a system otherwise dominated by officials. Its effectiveness depends on the relation between electoral choice and the conduct of government. Where citizens’ influence ends with the selection of representatives, their judgment is concentrated in occasional choices among people and programs prepared for them. Between elections, the priorities of government are worked out within institutions whose decisions they have little opportunity to direct. Where the ballot is citizens’ only instrument, it compensates for exclusion from governing rather than amounting to it.

Administration serves collective judgment when citizens retain effective control over its ends. Officials contribute the expertise and continuity needed to carry public decisions into practice. Displacement begins when that relationship changes: the officials responsible for implementation also acquire authority to determine the purposes of their work, while the public loses the means to redirect them. Under these conditions, technical decisions settle questions that should remain open to political judgment. Citizens encounter government chiefly as the recipients of decisions about their common life. The need for specialized administration has become a source of authority over the ends it was established to serve.

Weber frames the stakes sharply. Citizens are either “integrated into the state by making them its co-rulers,” or they are “administered” like a herd of cattle, “mere objects” in a bureaucratic state.19 His defense of parliamentarization gives political leadership and oversight a role in restraining officials.20 Their effectiveness depends on whether they preserve the public’s authority over administrative priorities. Where technical efficiency becomes the governing standard and that authority is absent, sustained contestation appears to administrators as interference with their work. The popular pressures Machiavelli treats as necessary to liberty are then judged by the disruption they cause. This treatment gives displacement a durable basis in the incentives of government. An institution organized to reward continuity and compliance has little internal reason to restore the authority of those it administers. Maintaining citizens’ influence requires political arrangements that keep public ends open to challenge and revision.

The difficulty also concerns the individual’s relation to institutions that exceed his understanding. In “The Metropolis and Mental Life,” Georg Simmel describes a social world organized through specialization, calculation, and increasingly impersonal relationships. The growth of objective culture outstrips the individual’s capacity to absorb it into his own experience. He becomes “a single cog as over against the vast overwhelming organization of things and forces.”21 Applied to politics, this disparity helps explain the distance between institutional capacity and personal influence. The individual depends on systems whose operation he grasps only in part, while the knowledge required to act within them becomes more specialized. Greater freedom in private life can coexist with diminished influence over the organization of common affairs. Political institutions must sustain effective judgment under these conditions.

Robert Dahl approaches this institutional problem by distinguishing democracy as an ideal from the regimes that approximate it. In Polyarchy, he observes that “no large system in the real world is fully democratized.”22 He calls existing regimes with extensive inclusion and public contestation polyarchies: systems that are “relatively (but incompletely) democratized,”23 with scope for further democratization. The distinction allows institutions to be assessed by the degree to which they make government responsive to citizens treated as political equals. Incompleteness becomes part of the assessment itself. A regime’s democratic achievements remain open to criticism even when it compares favorably with less inclusive and less competitive alternatives.

Dahl organizes this assessment along two dimensions, public contestation and the right to participate.24 The first concerns opportunities to oppose and challenge government; the second concerns who has access to those opportunities. Their relationship explains his warning that the development of public contestation is “not necessarily equivalent to full democratization,”25 since competition among a restricted group leaves the broader population excluded. Extending both dimensions gives more citizens a political voice and places rulers under greater pressure to respond. The present argument asks how far this voice reaches into the formation of public decisions. Access to competition matters through the authority it gives citizens over what government does.

The limits of responsiveness become visible where citizens influence decisions within an agenda they have little part in forming. Dahl distinguishes the ideal of equal responsiveness from the uneven opportunities for participation and contestation found across institutional arenas.26 That variation leaves room to examine the terms on which political choices reach the public. An electorate whose effective role is confined to approving or rejecting alternatives prepared elsewhere exercises a restricted form of judgment. Its opposition still carries consequences, including the defeat of proposals or the removal of representatives. The question is whether citizens also possess an enduring role in deciding which problems command attention and which alternatives deserve consideration. This is the further demand made by an account of agency grounded in collective authorship.

Dahl’s own preference is explicit. His conceptual scheme reflects a “commitment (or as others might see it, a bias) in favor of polyarchy as against less democratized regimes.”27 The institutions he describes secure valuable freedoms and means of opposition. They also provide a basis for further democratization. The stronger Aristotelian standard developed here places a further demand on these achievements: citizens must share authority over public purposes. Where that authority is withheld, freedom to oppose rulers leaves citizens’ exclusion from governing intact.

Schmitt’s account of plebiscitary democracy isolates the power to frame the public’s choices. In Legality and Legitimacy, he writes that the people “can only respond yes or no.”28 Those governing formulate the question; the people authorize or reject the answer presented to them. Popular sovereignty survives as a source of legitimacy while political initiative belongs to whoever determines the alternatives. Schmitt advances plebiscitary legitimacy against parliamentary legalism, yet the proposed recovery of political authority leaves this dependence in place. The public participates in a consequential decision whose terms originate beyond its own judgment. Popular authorization supplies legitimacy to decisions whose formation remains under the control of those governing.

Arendt examines the consequences of a public life from which consequential action has receded. In her discussion of Hobbes and bourgeois society in The Origins of Totalitarianism, public affairs appear to the politically deprived individual “in the guise of necessity,” intensifying his concern with private life and personal fate.29 Exclusion from the conduct of common affairs weakens his connection with fellow citizens. He comes to judge his position through comparison and competition with others. The conditions under which people act together have changed: common affairs confront them as something already organized, while their own efforts are absorbed into the pursuit of private advantage. This account brings the consequences of political exclusion into the relations through which individuals understand their lives.

Her later analysis of rightlessness carries the loss of consequential action to an extremity. Arendt writes that “the fundamental deprivation of human rights is manifested first and above all in the deprivation of a place in the world which makes opinions significant and actions effective.”30 A person excluded from political community loses the setting in which others respond to what he says and does. His treatment ceases to depend on his actions, leaving him subject to decisions over which he has no standing to exercise influence. The deprivation concerns the relation between a person and a common world that gives his conduct political consequence.

In this extremity, the right to action and opinion is lost, and treatment depends on accident, charity, or administrative discretion.31 Arendt’s stateless and rightless persons must be distinguished from citizens who retain legal protections, elections, and avenues of contestation. Those citizens still possess means of political action that the rightless have lost. The comparison concerns the importance of an effective place in public life. Democratic exhaustion describes the narrowing of that place within an enduring constitutional order, where political membership survives and the authority exercised through it diminishes. Arendt’s analysis clarifies why the consequences of judgment and action matter to the substance of membership, while the historical and legal differences limit the comparison.

The accounts examined here identify several ways in which citizens lose effective authority over common affairs. Office becomes a possession, opposition loses its institutional consequences, or administrative expertise acquires power over public purposes. Across these differences, the governing criterion remains the authority citizens exercise through participation. Administration supports self-government when it carries out ends that remain subject to effective public direction. Where officials or political leaders control those ends and restrict citizens to responding to choices already framed for them, participation ceases to sustain a shared practice of rule.

Representative government presents a substantial objection to this diagnosis. Through elections, delegation, and oversight, citizens govern indirectly; specialized administration gives their decisions a practical reach that direct participation alone could never provide. An account of political agency must recognize these achievements. The test is whether citizens retain effective means of revising priorities and redirecting the institutions acting in their name. Their responsibility for judgment need not extend to every administrative decision. It does require authority over the public purposes those decisions serve. Democratic exhaustion arises where the channels of representation and challenge continue to operate while that authority is lost.

Tocqueville’s jury and Schmitt’s plebiscite make the distinction concrete. Jury service gives citizens responsibility for judgment, together with the difficult task of learning from expert authority. The plebiscite places the initiative with those who formulate the question. Weber explains the institutional pressures that make control over officials necessary, while Simmel describes the growing disparity between the individual and the organized world. Democratic rule rests on citizens’ continuing authority to shape public purposes and hold those who carry them out to account.

The endurance of democratic institutions therefore leaves the endurance of democratic rule to be established. Elections and representation sustain political agency where citizens retain authority to contest public ends and redirect the institutions acting in their name. Once that authority is lost, participation increasingly authorizes decisions whose purposes have been determined elsewhere. Democratic exhaustion names this separation between political membership and the exercise of judgment that makes governing a shared responsibility. Its persistence depends on institutions that continue to secure procedural legitimacy after the public’s governing authority has diminished. Under those conditions, democracy endures without ruling.
